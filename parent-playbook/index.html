Page 6: The CLEAR Method

Part 3 | Use the GUS approach

CLEAR gives families and support teams five moves to use when a student is stuck. You do not need all five every time — most hard moments only need the first one. These five ideas run underneath the whole guide, so each one points you to the part of the book that goes further.

C — Correct

Correct the story before you correct your child. Name the exact thing that went wrong — the behavior, the skill, or the moment — without letting it turn into who your child is. “You are disorganized” becomes “The work got finished and never uploaded.” A missed assignment is a missed assignment; it is not proof of a character flaw. Say it warmly, say it clearly, and say it in words your child can actually take in. Done right, a correction teaches. Done wrong, it only discourages.

Goes deeper: Part 2 gives you the neutral-observation scripts for this.

L — Learn

Learn your child’s pattern before you try to change it. Every child has conditions where a skill holds up and conditions where it falls apart. Watch for the strengths, the triggers, the tools they will actually use, and the times of day when the same task is easy or impossible. Two weeks of noticing beats two months of guessing. “Homework after a snack and a shower goes fine. Homework straight off the bus ends in an argument.” That is not a preference — that is information, and you build the routine around it.

Goes deeper: Part 1 names the skills to watch for; Part 10 turns what you notice into a plan.

E — Encourage

Encourage the effort, the strategy, and the recovery — not the outcome. On any given day the grade is largely outside your child’s control. What they controlled is what you name. Point to the specific move that can be repeated: asking for help, opening the assignment before they felt ready, setting the timer, coming back after a mistake. “You got frustrated, you took five minutes, and you came back and finished it. That is the part I want you to remember.” Encouragement that names a repeatable action teaches something. “Good job” does not.

Goes deeper: Part 6 has the exact words for recognizing effort in the moment.

Page 9: Three Routines That Reduce Daily Conflict

Part 5 | Make home easier

1. The two-minute launch

  1. Name one task — not the whole evening.
  2. Open the right page, document, or app.
  3. Pick a first action so small it takes two minutes or less.
  4. Start a short timer and begin before discussing anything else.

Parent: “You do not have to finish the whole assignment right now. Let’s open it, read the directions, and pick the first step.”

2. The evening reset

  • Check tomorrow’s schedule and how everyone is getting where they need to go.
  • Put whatever is needed by the door or in the backpack tonight.
  • Charge devices in one consistent spot.
  • Write tomorrow’s first task on a note your child will actually see.

3. The weekly preview

  • Look at school, sports, appointments, and family plans together.
  • Spot the one busy day and plan for it before it arrives.
  • Break long-term work down to the next visible step.
  • Let your child choose when and where they can; you hold the boundaries that matter.

Keep it light: A ten-minute routine that actually happens beats a perfect forty-minute system everyone avoids. Pick one of these three and run it for two weeks before adding another.

Page 10: Conversation Scripts for Hard Moments

Part 6 | Talk so the student can participate

When your child is overwhelmed

Parent: “I can see this feels like too much. Do you need help getting calm, getting clear, or getting started?”

When work is missing

Parent: “Let’s solve one thing at a time. Was it never started, unfinished, finished but not turned in, or turned in to the wrong place?”

When you hear “I’ll do it later”

Parent: “That works if we name the time. Is it right after dinner or right after practice? Whichever you pick, I will not bring it up again until then.”

When your child refuses help

Parent: “I respect that you want space. I still need to know the plan. Would you rather show me the first step now or check back in ten minutes?”

When something big is due

Parent: “We are not planning the whole project tonight. What has to be done by Thursday for the rest of it to be doable?”

When a plan did not work

Parent: “The plan failed; you are not a failure. What should we keep, change, or drop next time?”

When effort deserves recognition

Parent: “You started before you felt ready and you used the checklist without an argument. That is the part that moved you forward.”

Avoid the lecture loop: Use fewer words, ask one question at a time, and then wait. Ten quiet seconds is not defiance — it is your child thinking.
